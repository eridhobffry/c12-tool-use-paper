## Core argument

Eridho's draft, with the fixes applied in the outline sentences. He still owns the prose wording in the paper body.

1. ReAct's benefit depends on the task. On PaLM-540B (Table 1), ReAct beats chain-of-thought on FEVER (60.9 vs. 56.3 accuracy) and trails it on HotpotQA (27.4 vs. 29.4 exact match).
2. The best combination order also depends on the task. HotpotQA: ReAct then CoT-SC, 35.1 exact match. FEVER: CoT-SC then ReAct, 64.6 accuracy. Both beat CoT-SC alone (33.4 exact match / 60.4 accuracy).
3. Toolformer's Table 9, on the T-REx subset of LAMA, shows diminishing returns. Raising the share of examples with a call from 82.8% to 98.1% raises accuracy by 0.6 percentage points (52.9 to 53.5).
4. Selective calling has a mechanism in each paper, not only a diminishing-returns curve. ReAct's combinations switch method by a rule: a step limit is reached, or the majority vote is low. Toolformer at k=1 is calibrated: accuracy on examples with no call is 44.3, against 34.9 when calls are disabled entirely. That gap is gone at higher k.
5. Neither paper establishes cost-effectiveness. Latency, tokens, and money are not measured.
6. The user-facing implication belongs in the Conclusion, tied to the fix/break point: more tool use is not necessarily better tool use, and a higher accuracy score does not by itself say what the user gained. Users wait for every call and pay for it. One sentence in the introduction, one in the Discussion or Conclusion. Modern chatbots (Grok, Claude Code) may appear as motivation only. No latency numbers: the papers' 2022–23 models are not today's systems.

HotpotQA is scored by exact match, FEVER by accuracy. Differences are percentage points.

## Table 9 reanalysis

T-REx, a LAMA subset. AC is accuracy on examples with a call. NC is accuracy on examples without one. k means the model may start a call when the call token is among its top-k next tokens. Toolformer allows at most one call per input in the evaluation, so "more calls" here means a higher share of examples with a call. It does not mean more tokens, latency, or money.

| k | % with call | All | AC | NC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0 | 34.9 | – | 34.9 |
| 1 | 40.3 | 47.8 | 53.0 | 44.3 |
| 3 | 82.8 | 52.9 | 58.0 | 29.0 |
| 10 | 98.1 | 53.5 | 54.0 | 22.5 |

WebQS: k=3 to k=10 raises the call rate from 99.3% to 100%, and accuracy stays at 26.3.

- Evidence. k 1 to 3: call rate +42.5 percentage points, accuracy +5.1. k 3 to 10: call rate +15.3 percentage points, accuracy +0.6.
- Interpretation, composition effect. AC falls from 58.0 to 54.0 because the questions that newly receive a call at k=10 are harder. "All" is the weighted average of AC and NC. The drop does not show that calls got worse.
- Eridho's inference, labeled as his, not the paper's. On the 15.3% of questions newly called at k=10, accuracy is about 29.8 without a call and about 32.4 with one, so about +2.5 percentage points. Write "about +2.5", not "+2.55". Rounding is amplified about six times. The figure is a net, so fixes and breaks cannot be separated.
  - With a call: (98.1 × 54.0 − 82.8 × 58.0) / 15.3
  - Without a call: (17.2 × 29.0 − 1.9 × 22.5) / 15.3
  - Assumption: every question called at k=3 is still called at k=10 with the same outcome (that subset still scores 58.0), and the 1.9% never called also scored 22.5 at k=3. The paper does not confirm this.
  - Rounding: with ±0.05 on each of the six rounded inputs, the difference ranges from about +1.7 to +3.4 (recomputed 21 Nov 2026).

## Settled concepts

- Cost is not one number. Money, tokens, number of calls, latency, and compute each need their own measure. An API price is not total cost.
- Fewer samples do not state total cost. Total cost is the number of samples times the cost per sample, and the cost per sample varies. Fig. 2 shows sample efficiency.
- Marginal cost per extra correct answer depends on what a correct answer is worth and on how reliable the gain is. A gain cannot be called "worth it" without that. Hypothetical illustration only, not from either paper: 33.4 to 35.1 at 40% more cost is about €2.35 per extra correct answer if the baseline costs €100 per 1,000 questions. Keep this out of the paper unless he marks it as hypothetical.
- Net accuracy hides the fix/break pattern. Moving from 60 to 80 by fixing 20 and breaking none is a different outcome from fixing 30 and breaking 10. Terms: regression rate, recovery rate, answer churn. Answer churn is how many answers change. It is not consistency across repeated runs. At equal accuracy and unknown cost, the method that breaks nothing is the better default. The other wins only if its fixes are worth more than its breaks. Neither paper reports a paired fix/break table.
- Toolformer's loss filter is not a correctness check and not a cost measure. In Table 10, the "Fast train success" search call is not a useful call and still lowers perplexity.
- ReAct Table 2. 6% of successful ReAct trajectories contain hallucinated reasoning or facts, so a right answer can rest on wrong reasoning. Among failures: reasoning errors 47% (including loops), search results with nothing useful 23%, hallucination 0% (chain-of-thought: 56%).
- k and the prediction loss are not cost measures.
- The step limit (7 on HotpotQA, 5 on FEVER) is a setting. The text says more steps did not help. Footnote 3 reports only that 0.84% (HotpotQA) and 1.33% (FEVER) of correct trajectories use all 7/5 steps. No average step count or step distribution is reported.
- ReAct can chain calls until it answers or hits the limit. A chain is not a guarantee: it can loop without recovering.
- Toolformer has no training examples of chained calls (§7), and the evaluation allowed at most one call per input. Do not write "the model can only make one call."
- The WikiText perplexity move from 9.9 to 10.3, and the multilingual QA drops, come from fine-tuning on CCNet. Toolformer with tools disabled matches GPT-J + CC at 10.3. Turning tools on helps in every language. Do not list these as downsides of tool use.
- Never claim either paper proves cost-effectiveness or economic efficiency.
- A "sweet spot" such as 2 vs. 5 vs. 14 calls is not tested. It can appear as an implication in the Discussion or as future work in the Conclusion. It is not a finding.
- Diet and exam analogies stay out of the paper. The exam analogy is fine for the talk.

### SMART, verified

- Citation: Cheng Qian, Emre Can Acikgoz, Hongru Wang, Xiusi Chen, Avirup Sil, Dilek Hakkani-Tür, Gokhan Tur, and Heng Ji. SMART: Self-Aware Agent for Tool Overuse Mitigation. Findings of ACL 2025. arXiv:2502.11435. Anthology page numbers were not checked. Do not invent them.
- The HTML page links the code at https://github.com/qiancheng0/Open-SMARTAgent. The repository contents were not read.
- Section 3 (Preliminaries) defines tool overuse: excessive reliance on external tools when the agent could have completed the task from parametric knowledge alone. The tool-overuse rate is α · β. α is the share of questions answered correctly with no tools. β is the share of those questions on which the model still makes at least one call.
- Use SMART only for that definition, if a sentence needs a name for calling a tool on a question the model can already answer. Do not import SMARTAgent's reported 24% reduction in tool use or 37% performance gain. Those results are about a different training setup, not about ReAct or Toolformer.

### Toolformer model size, now verified

Section 4.4 and Figure 4. The approach is applied to GPT-J and to GPT-2 models at 124M, 355M, 775M, and 1.6B parameters, using the question-answering tool, the calculator, and Wikipedia search. The paper says the ability to use the tools emerges at around 775M parameters, and that smaller models reach similar performance with and without tools. The stated exception is Wikipedia search on the QA benchmarks, which the authors call comparably easy to use (their word is "comparably").
